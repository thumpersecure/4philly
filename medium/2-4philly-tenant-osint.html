4Philly: The Free Tool That Lets Philadelphia Tenants Check a Building's L&I and 311 History Before They Sign a Lease

Most renters find out about a building's problems after they move in. 4Philly flips that — putting Philadelphia's public property, Licenses & Inspections, and 311 records into one searchable place, built by a tenant who learned the hard way why that information matters.

Every year, thousands of Philadelphia renters sign a lease based on a tour, a listing photo, and a leasing agent's smile. What they almost never see is the building's paper trail: the open Licenses & Inspections (L&I) violations, the expired rental license, the pattern of 311 complaints, the ownership entity behind the "luxury" branding. That information is public. It is also scattered across separate city portals that most people never think to check — and would struggle to navigate if they did.

4Philly exists to close that gap. It is a free, open tool that pulls Philadelphia's public property and code-enforcement records into one place, so a renter can look up an address and see the building's actual history before signing, not after.

Why a Tenant Built This

4Philly was not built by a startup chasing a market. It was built by a tenant who discovered, in the middle of a housing crisis of his own, that the single most important facts about his building were sitting in public databases the entire time — an expired rental license, a documented enforcement history — and that no ordinary renter would ever know to assemble them. The lesson was simple and infuriating: the information that could protect you exists, but the friction of finding it is doing the landlord's work for them.

So the tool was designed around one question: what would I have wanted to know before I moved in?

What 4Philly Actually Checks

4Philly draws on Philadelphia's open-data ecosystem to surface the records that matter most to a prospective or current tenant:

  • Rental license status. Under Philadelphia code, a landlord generally cannot legally collect rent or pursue eviction without a valid rental license. An expired or missing license is one of the most consequential — and most overlooked — facts about a building.
  • L&I violations and cases. The count and nature of code-enforcement violations, including serious categories like "unfit structure" and fire-safety failures.
  • Property and ownership records. Who actually owns the building behind the management brand.
  • 311 and complaint patterns. The texture of what residents have already reported.

Put together, these turn a leasing decision from a guess into an informed one.

Why This Is Tenant OSINT

Open-source intelligence — OSINT — usually gets discussed in the context of investigators and analysts. But the same principle applies to renters: the public record already contains the answers, and the advantage goes to whoever assembles it first. Corporate landlords have teams, lawyers, and data. An individual tenant has a phone and, until now, no easy way to see the same picture. 4Philly is an attempt to hand that picture back to the person who needs it most, at the exact moment it matters — before the signature.

How to Use It

The workflow is deliberately simple: enter a Philadelphia address, and 4Philly returns the building's public record in a readable form. No account, no fee, no catch. It is meant to take sixty seconds and to be usable by someone who has never heard the phrase "open data" in their life.

The Bigger Point

Housing accountability in a city like Philadelphia often fails not because the information is secret, but because it is fragmented. Splitting the rental-license portal from the L&I portal from the 311 system means that, in practice, the full story of a building is available to almost no one. Tools like 4Philly are a small correction to that asymmetry — a way of saying that if the record is public, it should also be legible.
