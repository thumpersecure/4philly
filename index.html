License Gap Scanner
Finds Philadelphia rental properties whose license has expired right now — a status the City's own map tools (Atlas, Property History) won't show for up to 60 days.
What "expired" means here, in plain terms
Philadelphia law is specific: under Code § 9-3902(1)(a), a landlord may not collect rent on a property that requires a rental license unless a valid license is currently in force. Under § 9-3901(4)(e), an owner who lets that lapse cannot collect rent or evict for nonpayment during the period of non-compliance.

Every rental license carries two dates. The expiration date is the one with legal weight — it's when that authority to collect rent ends. About 60 days later, the license is reclassified "Inactive" — an internal bookkeeping change, not a legal deadline.

The City's own map tools publish the second date, not the first. For the full 60-day window between them, a lapsed license shows no expiration date and a status that hasn't yet flipped to "Inactive" — so the property looks unremarkable on the map even though, right now, it isn't legally licensed to collect rent.

This scanner queries that same City data directly for every Rental-type license currently sitting in that window — status Expired, expiration date already passed, not yet reclassified. It reads the City's independently-verified, correctly time-zoned copy of the data (a separate, confirmed bug shifts the same date a calendar day early on the City's map-facing copy — not used here).

This is public licensing information, not legal advice. A property here means its rental license shows expired in the City's own daily-refreshed data as of the scan time — confirm specifics for any one address with a full lookup before relying on it.

Where do these numbers come from, and why?
Same source as the city scanner: Philadelphia's open data on Carto (phl.carto.com), filtered to the ZIP you enter. Violations and licenses are matched by OPA account number; 311 complaints are matched by ZIP code on the complaint record (the 311 dataset has no OPA number, so it is joined by location, not parcel ID).

Why it can differ from a property lookup: this reads the Carto historical mirror, which lags L&I's live Eclipse backend that the property lookup uses. Numbers here can be days-to-weeks behind — use the ZIP scan to spot problem properties in an area, then confirm any one of them with a lookup against the live data. Government, institutional, and hotel owners are filtered out.

Know the OPA number? Search by it instead (more precise — optional)

An OPA number (Office of Property Assessment account number) is the city's unique ID for a single parcel of land — like a fingerprint for the property. Every property in Philadelphia has one.

Why it can beat the address: one street address can cover several parcels (a corner lot, a subdivided building, a condo with many units), and the same address can be written many ways (“1500 Market St” vs the city's official “1500-16 MARKET ST”). The OPA number points at exactly one parcel with no ambiguity, so if you have it, the lookup is guaranteed to hit the right record. Don't have it? Just use the address — that's what most people do, and this tool figures out the matching OPA for you. You can find a property's OPA number on Atlas or a tax bill.
